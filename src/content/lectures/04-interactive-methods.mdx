A system is only as good as the understanding it was built on, and that
understanding starts with **requirements discovery** — the work of finding out
what the system must do and how well it must do it. This lesson covers the
methods that involve talking to people directly: interviews, listening for
workplace stories, running a joint design workshop, and sending out
questionnaires. (The methods that do not interrupt anyone — sampling, reading
records, observation — are the [next lesson](../05-unobtrusive-methods).)

## What a requirement is

A **system requirement** (or business requirement) is a statement of something
the system must do or a quality it must have. They split into two kinds.

**Functional requirements** describe a function the system must perform: "the
system shall produce a monthly aging report", "a clerk can void a transaction
before it is posted". If you can point to a feature that does it, it is
functional.

**Nonfunctional requirements** describe how well, or under what constraints, the
system must operate: performance, capacity, availability, security, usability,
maintainability, legal and policy limits. They are easy to leave out because no
single screen "does" them, and expensive to add later.

### Getting them right matters

A wrong or vague requirement propagates. The classic example: "create a means to
transport a single individual from home to work" — that could be a car, a
bicycle, or a helicopter, and each costs wildly different amounts. Incorrect
requirements mean the system costs more than projected, arrives late, does not do
what users need, and gets patched repeatedly after delivery.

Good requirements are **consistent** (they do not contradict each other),
**complete**, **feasible**, genuinely **required** (not gold-plating),
**accurate**, **traceable** back to a business need, and **verifiable** — you can
test whether each one was met. The whole set is written up in a **requirements
definition document**, reviewed with stakeholders (**validation**), and then kept
under **requirements management** so that changes are controlled rather than
absorbed silently.

## Interviewing

An interview collects information about requirements, and also about opinions,
feelings, and goals that never show up in a document. It has to be prepared:
read the background material first, set clear objectives for the conversation,
decide who to interview and in what order, and plan the questions.

### Two kinds of question

**Open-ended questions** put no limit on the answer — "walk me through how you
handle a return". They relax the interviewee, surface vocabulary and priorities
you did not know to ask about, and keep the conversation alive. The cost is time,
irrelevant detail, and the risk of losing control of the interview.

**Closed questions** limit the responses — "how many returns per day?", "do you
approve refunds yourself, yes or no?". They are fast, easy to compare across
interviewees, and keep the interview on track, but they are dull to answer and
miss the detail nobody thought to ask for.

Two more types are worth naming. **Bipolar questions** are a closed question with
exactly two answers — yes/no, agree/disagree — and should be used sparingly.
**Probes** are follow-ups — "why?", "can you give an example?" — that pull more
detail out of a previous answer; they are how you turn a thin response into a
useful one.

### Ordering the questions

The sequence of questions has structure, and there are three standard shapes.

A **pyramid** starts narrow and opens out: specific closed questions first,
broadening to open-ended ones. Use it when the interviewee needs warming up, or
when you want them thinking about a topic before being asked to reflect on it
broadly.

  caption="Pyramid: start with specific questions, widen to general ones."

/>

A **funnel** does the opposite: broad open-ended questions first, narrowing to
specific closed ones. Use it when the interviewee already has strong opinions and
wants to vent them before being pinned down.

<Figure
  src="lec04/funnel-structure.png"
  alt="An inverted-funnel diagram for interview question order, wide at the top and narrow at the bottom, with example questions moving from 'what are your reactions to the new web-based procurement system?' at the top to 'is there any essential item that has been excluded from the site?' at the bottom."
  caption="Funnel: start with broad questions, narrow to specific ones."

A **diamond** combines both: specific, then broad, then specific again. It starts
concrete to build confidence, opens into the big issues, then closes on
particulars — the most engaging structure for a long interview, at the cost of
taking the longest.

### Closing and recording

End every interview by asking "is there anything else you would like to add?",
then summarise what you heard and confirm it. Write the **interview report** as
soon as possible afterwards — a short summary first, then detail — and send it
back to the interviewee to check.

## Stories

People in a workplace tell **stories**, and those stories carry requirements that
no direct question elicits. They come in four kinds: **experiential** (what the
teller did), **explanatory** (why something is the way it is), **validating** (a
story told to justify a decision), and **prescriptive** (a story told to tell you
how things should be done). Collecting them is a low-effort way to learn what a
process actually feels like from inside.

## Joint Application Design

**Joint Application Design** (JAD) replaces a series of one-on-one interviews with
a single structured workshop. It fits when users are impatient for change, the
culture supports group work, and the analysts expect the traditional
interview-by-interview approach to drag.

The room needs specific roles: an **executive sponsor** with authority, an **IS
analyst**, the **users**, a neutral **session leader**, **observers**, and a
**scribe** to capture decisions. Meetings are usually held offsite, in
comfortable surroundings, scheduled when users can actually be away from their
desks.

JAD done well saves time, speeds development, and produces stronger user
ownership of the result. It fails when the block of time cannot be freed for
everyone, when preparation is thin, or when the session is not skilfully
facilitated.

## Questionnaires

A **questionnaire** trades the depth of an interview for reach. It is the right
tool when the people you need to hear from are geographically spread out, when
many people are involved and you want a statistically meaningful picture, when
you are exploring before deciding what to pursue in interviews, or when you want
to surface problems that people will admit on paper but not in person.

### Question types and language

Questionnaire questions are **open-ended** or **closed**, with the same trade-off
as in interviews — but on a questionnaire you cannot probe, so you must
anticipate the range of answers a question will produce before you send it.

The wording must be **simple, specific, short, not patronising, free of bias**,
and aimed at people who actually know the answer.

### Measurement scales

Closed questions often use a **scale**. Two forms matter here. **Nominal** scales
just classify — "which department are you in?" — and are the weakest measurement:
you can count categories and nothing more. **Interval** scales assume equal gaps
between points — a 1-to-5 agreement scale — with no true zero, which lets you
compare and average responses.

A scale should be **reliable** (the same respondent gives the same answer on a
second sitting) and **valid** (it measures what you meant it to). Three biases
recur: **leniency**, where easy raters push everything high — countered by moving
the neutral point off centre; **central tendency**, where everyone rates
everything average — countered by widening the differences between points; and
the **halo effect**, where an impression from one question bleeds into the next —
countered by putting one trait per page or spacing related questions apart.

### Layout and delivery

Give the form ample white space and room to write, make it easy to read, put the
most important questions first, cluster related questions, and introduce
sensitive items only after easier ones. Web surveys add a control question:
which input control captures each answer.

Questionnaires can be gathered by convening respondents together, handing them
out in person, mailing them, or submitting them electronically — the last being
cheapest and easiest to tally, since results arrive already stored.

## Where this idea shows up now

The formats have moved online — user interviews over video, JAD as a design
sprint or workshop, questionnaires as web forms and in-product surveys — but the
craft is unchanged. You still choose open versus closed deliberately, still order
questions to warm a person up or let them vent first, still write survey items
that are short and unbiased, and still design around leniency, central tendency,
and the halo effect. Anticipating the range of answers before you ship a survey
is exactly the work of writing good analytics events or a well-scoped user study.
