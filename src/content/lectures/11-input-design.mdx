**Input** is how data gets into the system, and it sets a hard ceiling on
everything downstream: the quality of the output can never exceed the quality of
the input. Input design is about capturing each fact accurately, once, with as
little effort and as little chance of error as possible.

## Objectives

Good input design aims for:

- **Effectiveness** — it serves a specific purpose.
- **Accuracy** — it is hard to enter something wrong.
- **Ease of use** — it is straightforward and not tiring.
- **Consistency** — similar inputs are captured the same way everywhere.
- **Simplicity** — it keeps attention on the task.
- **Attractiveness** — people are willing to use it.

## Paper form design

Even now, a lot of data starts life on a paper form, and a badly designed one
produces bad data.

### Make it easy to fill in

**Form flow** should follow how a person reads — left to right and top to bottom —
and group related fields so the eye and the pen move as little as possible.

A form has **seven sections**: the **heading** (what the form is), **identification
and access** (form number, who may use it), **instructions**, the **body** (the
main fields), **signature and verification**, **totals**, and **comments**. Not
every form needs all seven, but knowing the list stops you from forgetting one.

### Captioning

The **caption** tells the person what a field is for, and there are several
styles.

The options include a **line caption** (label beside the blank), a **boxed
caption** (label inside the field), a **table caption** (column headings over
rows of blanks), and **checklists** laid out vertically or horizontally for
choosing among alternatives. Consistency across the form matters more than which
style you pick.

### The other goals

Beyond easy completion, a form must **meet its intended purpose** (specialty
forms — multi-part, continuous-feed, machine-readable — for specific needs),
**ensure accurate completion** (layout and instructions that make errors hard),
and **stay attractive** (uncluttered, so people are drawn into completing it).
Computer-assisted form design tools, and tools that scan an existing paper form
and turn its regions into fillable fields, speed this up. Forms in use should be
**controlled** — each one still serving its purpose, none duplicating another.

## Display and web forms

On screen, the guidelines converge with output design: keep the **display
simple** (heading, body, comments and instructions — nothing more), keep it
**consistent** (the same information in the same place on every screen),
**facilitate movement** (the three-clicks rule — any screen reachable in about
three clicks), and make it **attractive** enough to hold attention. **Icons** —
small pictorial symbols for actions — help when they are recognisable, and hurt
when they are guesswork.

## GUI controls

The core of input design on screen is choosing the right **control** for each
field.

/>

- **Text box** — free text. Size it to the expected content; caption to the left
  or above.
- **Check box** — a **non-exclusive** choice; label to the right. Several can be
  ticked at once.
- **Option / radio button** — an **exclusive** choice within a group; label to
  the right. Exactly one is selected.
- **List and drop-down list** — a choice from many options when space is tight;
  put a common default at the top.
- **Tab control** — one tab per feature area, most-used tabs first.
- **Slider and spin button** — a value on a continuous range.
- **Text area** — a larger block of text, scrollable.
- **Message box** — a warning or feedback in a dialog.
- **Command button** — an action: Submit, Clear, and so on.

<Note label="The exclusive/non-exclusive rule">
If the choices are mutually exclusive, use radio buttons or a drop-down. If more
than one can be true at once, use check boxes. Getting this wrong is the most
common input-control mistake.
</Note>

### Controls, values, and hidden fields

Every control **stores a value**; a web form submits those values as name/value
pairs. **Hidden fields** carry a name and value that the user never sees and that
take no space — used to pass state between pages. **Event-response charts** list
the events a page must handle and what each should do, which is how the page's
behaviour is planned.

**HTML5** adds input types that render as ordinary text boxes on a desktop but
become specialised controls on a phone or tablet: `placeholder` help text, a
`datalist` of suggestions, and dedicated date, time, email, and number controls
that summon the right keyboard.

## Colour

Colour is used to contrast foreground and background, highlight important fields,
flag errors, and group related items. The most legible foreground-on-background
combinations are black on yellow, green on white, blue on white, and white on
blue — and colour should never be the *only* channel carrying meaning.

## Web fill-in forms

For a web form: provide clear instructions, demonstrate a logical entry sequence,
include exactly two basic buttons — **Submit** and **Clear** — break a long form
into sections or pages, and validate as early as possible so the user learns
about a bad entry before they have moved on.

## Where this idea shows up now

The seven sections of a form and the exclusive/non-exclusive control rule are
unchanged — they are baked into every form library and design system. Client-side
validation, input masks, and typed HTML controls are the modern version of
"design the form so errors are hard to make". The one thing worth carrying
forward deliberately is the discipline of capturing each fact once, at its
source: every place a user re-keys something the system already knows is a place
errors are being manufactured.
