A [data flow diagram](../08-data-flow-diagrams) shows *that* a process exists and
what flows in and out of it. It does not show what the process actually does with
those inputs. That is the job of a **process specification** — and where the logic
is a decision, of a **decision table** or **decision tree**.

## Process specifications

A process specification (also called a **minispec**) is written for the
**primitive** processes on a DFD — the ones at the bottom level that are not
exploded any further — and sometimes for higher-level ones too. Its goals:

- **Reduce ambiguity.** Writing it forces the analyst to learn exactly how the
  process works, and often exposes that nobody actually knows.
- **Get a precise description** of what the process accomplishes, which the
  programmer can build from and the user can validate.
- **Check the logic** — a written specification can be inspected for gaps and
  contradictions in a way a verbal description cannot.

Specifications are **not** written for processes that are pure physical input or
output, or that are trivial reads and writes with no logic — there is nothing to
specify.

### What goes in one

A specification records: the **process number** (matching the DFD exactly, so the
two can be cross-checked), the **process name** (also matching the DFD), a plain
description of what the process accomplishes, the **input data flows** and
**output data flows** (using the names from the DFD and data dictionary), the
**type** of process (batch, online, or manual), whether it **uses pre-written
code** (name the subprogram), the **process logic** itself, a **reference** to a
fuller description if the logic does not fit, and a list of **unresolved issues**.

That last item matters: incomplete logic is written down as an open question, and
those questions become the agenda for the next interview.

### It is policy, not code

The logic description states **business rules and policy**, not programming
pseudo-code. Business rules take a few standard shapes: definitions of business
terms, conditions paired with the actions they trigger, data-integrity
constraints, and computations. "If the customer has ordered within twelve months,
waive the setup fee" is a business rule; `if (customer.lastOrder > cutoff)` is
not.

## Structured English

**Structured English** writes logic as a restricted subset of English with
explicit control structure. Use it when the logic involves formulas or iteration,
or when decisions are not complex enough to need a table.

Everything is expressed with four building blocks:

<Figure
  src="lec09/structured-english-types.png"
  alt="A table of structured English types with descriptions and examples. Sequential structure: a block of instructions with no branching (Action 1, Action 2, Action 3). Decision structure: IF condition A is true THEN implement action A ELSE implement action B ENDIF. Case structure: a special decision where cases are mutually exclusive, written as nested IF/ELSE with a final ELSE print error. Iteration: DO WHILE there are customers, Action 1, ENDDO."
  caption="The four structures of structured English, with an example of each."
  source="Lecture 9, slide 25"
/>

- **Sequence** — do this, then this, then this.
- **Decision** — `IF condition THEN action A ELSE action B ENDIF`.
- **Case** — a decision with several mutually exclusive branches and an
  `ELSE print error` fallthrough.
- **Iteration** — `DO WHILE ... ENDDO` for a block that repeats.

The **data dictionary** is the starting point: the data elements it defines are
the nouns the structured English operates on. Its advantages are that it removes
the ambiguity of ordinary language while staying readable by non-programmers, so
it works as a communication tool between analyst, user, and developer.

## Decision tables

When the logic is "which action, given this combination of conditions?", a
**decision table** lays out every combination explicitly. It has four quadrants.

<Figure
  src="lec09/decision-table-format.png"
  alt="The standard decision table layout: a two-by-two grid. Top-left quadrant is 'conditions', top-right is 'condition alternatives', bottom-left is 'actions', bottom-right is 'action entries'. Columns across the right side are labelled 'rules'."
  caption="The four quadrants: conditions and their alternatives on top, actions and their entries below, one rule per column."
  source="Lecture 9, slide 29"
/>

Top-left lists the **conditions**. Top-right holds the **condition alternatives** —
one column per rule, each column a specific combination of Y/N answers.
Bottom-left lists the **actions**. Bottom-right holds the **action entries** —
an `X` marking which actions fire for each rule.

<Figure
  src="lec09/checkout-decision-table.png"
  alt="A customer checkout decision table. Conditions: order under $50, pays by check with two forms of ID, uses a credit card — with Y/N combinations across four rules. Actions marked with X: complete the sale after verifying signature; complete the sale, no signature needed; call supervisor for approval; communicate electronically with the bank for credit card authorization."
  caption="A worked table: three conditions, four rules, one action per rule."
  source="Lecture 9, slide 30"
/>

### Building one, and checking it

The construction is mechanical: count the conditions (that sets the number of
condition rows), count the actions (action rows), count the alternatives per
condition, multiply the alternatives together to get the maximum number of rule
columns, then fill in the alternatives systematically and mark the actions.

Then check for four problems:

- **Incompleteness** — a combination of conditions with no rule.
- **Impossible situations** — a rule whose condition combination cannot occur in
  reality.
- **Contradictions** — two rules with the same conditions but different actions.
- **Redundancy** — two rules with different conditions but identical actions,
  which can often be **combined** by replacing the differing condition with a
  dash ("don't care").

The strength of a decision table is exactly this: completeness and errors are
easy to see because every case is a column.

## Decision trees

A **decision tree** shows the same kind of logic when the **order** in which
conditions are checked matters, or when the branching is deep. It is read left to
right: a node, a branch per condition alternative, then either another node or a
leaf that names the action.

<Figure
  src="lec09/decision-tree.png"
  alt="A decision tree for non-cash purchase approval at a department store. First split on order under $50 versus $50 or over. Under $50: split on check versus credit card, leading to 'complete the sale after verifying signature' or 'complete the sale, no signature needed'. $50 or over: split on check versus credit card, leading to 'call supervisor for approval' or 'communicate electronically with the bank for authorization'."
  caption="The same logic as a tree — the order of the splits is visible, which a table hides."
  source="Lecture 9, slide 46"
/>

Its advantage over a table is that the **sequence** of checks is immediately
visible, and conditions that only matter down one branch do not clutter the
others.

<Note label="Which to use">
Structured English when actions repeat and iteration or formulas dominate.
A decision table when you need to be sure every combination of conditions is
covered. A decision tree when the order of the decisions is itself part of the
logic.
</Note>

## Where this idea shows up now

A process specification is an acceptance criterion or a well-written ticket: what
the thing does, its inputs and outputs, and the open questions, in the language
of the business. Decision tables are still the right tool for pricing rules,
eligibility checks, and permission matrices — and they map directly onto the
parameterised test cases that verify that logic. Decision trees survive
literally, in rules engines and in the "if this, then that" branching of feature
flags and routing rules.
