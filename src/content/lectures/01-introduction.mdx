This course is about changing how an organisation works by changing the systems it
runs on — and doing that deliberately, with a method, rather than by guesswork.
This first lesson sets up the vocabulary the rest of the course assumes.

## What a system is

A **system** is a set of interrelated parts that work together toward a purpose,
where changing one part affects the others. A business is a system. The software
it runs is a system. A department, a warehouse, a checkout process — all systems,
nested inside larger ones.

Every system has the same anatomy: **inputs** enter it, **processes** transform
them, **outputs** leave it, a **boundary** separates it from everything else, and
that everything else is its **environment**. **Feedback** is output that comes
back as input and lets the system adjust itself.

The two properties that make systems thinking useful are **interrelatedness** —
you cannot change one part in isolation — and **purpose** — the system exists to
achieve something, and every part is judged by whether it serves that.

## Kinds of information system

Organisations run several categories of information system, and they stack by how
much judgement they support.

<Figure
  src="lec01/system-types-pyramid.png"
  alt="A pyramid of information system types. From the base up: transaction processing systems; office automation systems and knowledge work systems; management information systems and decision support systems; expert systems; and at the apex executive support systems (ESS), group decision support systems (GDSS), and computer-supported collaborative work systems (CSCWS)."
  caption="Information systems by the level of decision they support, transaction processing at the base."
  source="Lecture 1, slide 2"
/>

- **Transaction processing systems** (TPS) — record the day-to-day operations:
  orders, payments, shipments. High volume, structured, unglamorous, essential.
- **Office automation** and **knowledge work systems** — support the people who
  create and handle information: documents, spreadsheets, schedules, design
  tools.
- **Management information systems** (MIS) and **decision support systems** (DSS) —
  summarise operational data into reports, and let managers explore "what if"
  questions against it.
- **Expert systems** — capture the reasoning of a specialist and apply it to new
  cases.
- **Executive support**, **group decision support**, and **collaborative work
  systems** — help senior people and teams make unstructured, high-stakes
  decisions together.

Newer categories — enterprise systems, ecommerce, mobile and web systems — cut
across the pyramid rather than sitting at one level.

## The systems analyst

The **systems analyst** is the person who studies how an organisation works,
decides with its people what should change, and specifies the new system
precisely enough to build. The role sits between the users, who know the business
but not the technology, and the developers, who know the technology but not the
business.

An analyst plays three roles at once: **consultant** (an outside perspective on
the organisation's problems), **supporting expert** (technical knowledge the
organisation lacks), and **agent of change** (someone whose presence and
recommendations will alter how people work, whether or not a line of code is
written).

## The development life cycle

The work is structured as a **systems development life cycle** (SDLC) — a
sequence of phases, drawn as a loop because it repeats every time the system is
revised.

<Figure
  src="lec01/sdlc-phases.png"
  alt="A circular diagram of seven SDLC phases: 1 identifying problems, opportunities, and objectives; 2 determining human information requirements; 3 analysing system needs; 4 designing the recommended system; 5 developing and documenting software; 6 testing and maintaining the system; 7 implementing and evaluating the system."
  caption="The seven phases, as a cycle — the last phase feeds the first."
  source="Lecture 1, slide 4"
/>

1. **Identify problems, opportunities, and objectives** — decide what is worth
   working on and why.
2. **Determine human information requirements** — find out what the people
   involved actually need, using [interactive](../04-interactive-methods) and
   [unobtrusive](../05-unobtrusive-methods) methods.
3. **Analyse system needs** — model the current and proposed system with
   [data flow diagrams](../08-data-flow-diagrams),
   [UML](../07-uml), and [process specifications](../09-process-specifications).
4. **Design the recommended system** — the [output](../10-output-design) and
   [input](../11-input-design), the files, the interfaces, the controls.
5. **Develop and document software** — build it, and write down how it works.
6. **Test and maintain the system** — verify it, then keep it running.
7. **Implement and evaluate** — put it into use, train people, and check whether
   it met the objectives from phase 1.

The development project is the visible, expensive-looking part, but over a
system's life the accumulated cost of bug fixes, small enhancements, and
adapting to changes in the business and the technology is larger. Designing for
that tail — making the system understandable and changeable — is part of the job,
not an afterthought.

## Choosing an approach

The SDLC is not the only way to run the work. Three broad approaches, each suited
to different conditions:

<Figure
  src="lec01/choosing-an-approach.png"
  alt="A table with columns 'choose' and 'when'. The systems development life cycle (SDLC) approach: when systems have been built and documented with SDLC before, documentation of each step matters, upper management is comfortable with it, and there are adequate resources and time. Agile methodologies: when there is a champion of agile in the organisation, applications must be built quickly for a changing environment, a rescue is needed, the customer accepts incremental improvements, and executives and analysts agree with agile principles. Object-oriented methodologies: when the problems modelled fit into classes, the organisation supports UML learning, systems can be added one subsystem at a time, reuse of prior code is possible, and it is acceptable to tackle the hard problems first."
  caption="When to reach for the SDLC, an agile method, or an object-oriented approach."
  source="Lecture 1, slide 9"
/>

Use the **SDLC** when documentation and predictability matter, management expects
it, and there is time to do each phase properly. Use an
**[agile](../06-agile-and-prototyping) method** when speed and responsiveness to
change matter more than a full paper trail, and the customer will accept the
system growing incrementally. Use an **[object-oriented](../07-uml) approach**
when the domain decomposes naturally into classes, reuse is likely, and the
system can be delivered one subsystem at a time.

In practice these blend — an agile project still analyses requirements and
designs output; an SDLC project still iterates — but the framing helps you pick a
default.

## Where this idea shows up now

The seven phases still describe what any software project does, whether it calls
them phases or not: understand the problem, learn what people need, model it,
design it, build it, ship it, and see if it worked. The maintenance reality is
stronger than ever — most engineering effort is spent changing systems that
already exist — which is why the modern emphasis on readable code, tests, and
observability is really an emphasis on making that long tail survivable.
