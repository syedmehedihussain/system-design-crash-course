An analyst cannot improve an organisation they have not described. This lesson is
about that description — seeing an organisation as a system, understanding what
sits around it and what feeds back into it, and drawing the first models that
turn a vague sense of "how things work" into something you can reason about.

## The organisation as a system

An organisation takes **inputs** from its surroundings — materials, money,
people, information — runs **processes** on them, and sends **outputs** back out:
products, services, payments, information. Its **goals** steer which processes run
and how their results are judged.

  alt="A cloud shape labelled 'System', with 'Inputs' entering from the left and 'Outputs' leaving to the right, and a control loop at the top passing through a triangle labelled 'Goals'."
  caption="The organisation as a system: inputs transformed into outputs, with goals closing the loop."
  source="Lecture 2, slide 2"
/>

Everything the organisation does is nested. A department is a **subsystem** of the
organisation; the organisation is a subsystem of its industry. A change at one
level ripples up and down.

## Environment and boundaries

The **environment** is everything outside the system boundary that affects it or
is affected by it: customers, suppliers, competitors, regulators, the labour
market, the economy. Part of modelling an organisation is deciding exactly where
its boundary lies — what is inside the system you intend to change, and what is
an external party you can only send data to or receive data from. That decision
is made explicit in a **context-level model**, and it is the same decision the
[context diagram](../08-data-flow-diagrams) forces later.

## Feedback and control

A system stays on course by **feedback** — routing some of its output back as
input so it can measure results against goals and adjust. Feedback can be
internal, and it can cross subsystem boundaries: the output of one part becomes
the input of another.

<Figure
  src="lec02/feedback-and-environment.png"
  alt="A cloud labelled with a cycle between 'Production' and 'Marketing'. A note reads 'outputs from marketing become the inputs for production'; another reads 'outputs from production become the inputs for marketing'."
  caption="Feedback between subsystems: each one's output is another's input, and the environment supplies and absorbs both."
  source="Lecture 2, slide 3"
/>

An analyst looks for where feedback is missing — a process whose results nobody
measures — or where it is too slow to be useful.

## Interrelatedness

The defining property of a system is that its parts **depend on each other**, so
the same organisation looks different depending on which part you stand in.

<Figure
  src="lec02/interrelatedness-gears.png"
  alt="Two clusters of interlocking gears. One is captioned 'how a marketing manager may view the organization', the other 'how a production manager may view the organization' — the same gears, arranged with a different one at the centre."
  caption="The same organisation, centred differently: a marketing manager and a production manager see different systems."
  source="Lecture 2, slide 4"
/>

This is why an analyst interviews people in several roles before modelling
anything. Each person describes the organisation with themselves at the centre,
and the real system is the union of those views, not any one of them.

## Levels of management

Organisations make decisions at three levels, and each needs different
information from the same underlying data:

- **Operational** — the day-to-day. Decisions are frequent, structured, and based
  on detailed, current data. "Do we have this item in stock?"
- **Tactical (middle management)** — the medium term. Decisions are periodic and
  semi-structured, based on summarised data and short-term trends. "Should we
  reorder from this supplier?"
- **Strategic (senior management)** — the long term. Decisions are infrequent,
  unstructured, and draw on aggregated internal data plus a lot of external
  information. "Should we enter this market?"

A system that serves one level well can be useless to another. The detail an
operational clerk needs is noise to a strategic planner; the aggregate a planner
wants hides what the clerk must see.

## Entity-relationship diagrams

One of the first models an analyst draws is an **entity-relationship diagram**
(ERD) — the things the organisation keeps information about, and how they relate.

<Figure
  src="lec02/er-diagram.png"
  alt="An entity-relationship diagram: an 'Employee' box and a 'Phone Extension' box connected by a relationship, read as 'one employee is assigned to one phone extension' and 'a phone extension is listed for an employee'."
  caption="An ERD: entities as boxes, a named relationship between them, and the multiplicity on each end."
  source="Lecture 2, slide 7"
/>

An **entity** is something the organisation stores data about — a person, place,
thing, or event (employee, department, order). A **relationship** is a meaningful
association between entities, named as a verb phrase and read in both directions.
The marks on each end give the **multiplicity** — one-to-one, one-to-many,
many-to-many.

To build one: list the entities to understand the organisation, choose the key
entities that keep the problem to a manageable size, identify the primary entity,
and confirm all of it against what interviews and documents told you. The ERD
becomes the backbone of the [class diagram](../07-uml) and of the data stores on
a [data flow diagram](../08-data-flow-diagrams).

<Note label="Why model first">
The models are not deliverables for their own sake. Drawing a context view forces
a decision about scope; drawing an ERD forces agreement about what the
organisation's core "things" even are. Disagreements surface on the whiteboard
instead of in the built system.
</Note>

## Where this idea shows up now

"The organisation as a system with an environment" is now called domain modelling
or context mapping, and the boundary decision is the same one every service
design starts from. The three levels of management map onto the different
audiences a modern data platform serves — operational dashboards, analytics, and
executive reporting off one warehouse. And the ERD never left: it is the entity
model behind every database schema and API design.
