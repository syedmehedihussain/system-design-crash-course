Interviews and questionnaires ask people what they do. **Unobtrusive methods**
find out without asking — by sampling records, reading the documents an
organisation already produces, and watching the workplace. They are less
disruptive, they catch things people would not think to mention or would rather
not admit, and they are insufficient on their own: they work as a check on, and a
complement to, the interactive methods in the [previous lesson](../04-interactive-methods).

## Sampling

**Sampling** is systematically selecting a representative subset of a population
so you can study the subset instead of the whole. An analyst samples to contain
cost, speed up data gathering, improve effectiveness (a careful look at a good
sample beats a rushed look at everything), and reduce bias (asking a few people
chosen well is less skewed than asking whoever is available).

Designing a sample is four decisions: what data to collect, from which population,
what **type** of sample, and how large.

### Types of sample

- **Convenience** — take whatever is easiest to reach. Unrestricted and
  non-probability; the least reliable.
- **Purposive** — pick, by judgement, a group that looks knowledgeable and
  relevant. Still non-probability, but the judgement is deliberate.
- **Simple random** — every element has an equal chance of selection.
- **Complex random** — the kind an analyst should prefer when possible.
  Three variants: **systematic** (take every *n*th record), **stratified**
  (sample separately within meaningful subgroups so each is represented), and
  **cluster** (sample whole groups, then everything within the chosen groups).

### Sample size

The size needed is driven by how confident you want to be and how much error you
will tolerate. The procedure: identify the attribute you are measuring (say,
order forms containing a mistake), find the records that hold it, estimate the
proportion of the population that has the attribute, choose a confidence level
and an acceptable interval, and compute the size from the standard error of the
proportion. The point is not the formula but the mindset — a sample big enough to
support the claim you intend to make, and no bigger.

## Reading the records

**Investigation** is discovery and analysis of **hard data**, which comes in two
kinds.

### Quantitative documents

These carry numbers: **reports used for decision making** (sales, production,
summary), **performance reports**, **records** that give periodic updates on the
business, and **data-capture forms**. For forms specifically: collect every form
in use — official and "bootleg" ones people made themselves — note each form's
type and intended distribution, and then ask hard questions of the filled-in
copies. Is the form completed in full, or are fields habitually left blank? Are
there forms nobody uses? Are official forms being bypassed by unofficial ones?
Blank fields and bootleg forms are strong signals that the real process differs
from the documented one.

### Qualitative documents

These carry meaning rather than counts: email and memos, notices on bulletin
boards, the corporate website, manuals, policy handbooks. You read them for the
**guiding metaphors** the organisation uses to describe itself ("we run a tight
ship", "the pipeline"), an **insiders-versus-outsiders** mentality, what counts
as success or failure, and what is treated as connected to what. A memo's
language reveals how the organisation actually thinks, which is often not what its
mission statement says.

## Observation

**Observation** shows what people actually do, as opposed to what they report
doing — the informal relationships, the shortcuts, the steps that got dropped.

Two structured approaches are worth naming. The **analyst's playscript** records a
decision-maker's actions in script form: an actor column and an action column,
each line a single observed action ("reads production report", "calls suppliers
to discuss deviations"). It captures a role as a sequence of concrete acts.

**STROBE** — STRuctured OBservation of the Environment — reads the decision-maker's
physical surroundings for evidence that confirms or contradicts what they said in
an interview. Its elements: office location (who has the corner office; are key
people dispersed?), desk placement (does it invite conversation, or signal
power?), stationary equipment (does this person gather and keep information
personally?), props (a PC, phone, tablet in use?), external information sources
(trade journals, outside feeds?), lighting and colour (set up for detailed work
or for casual talk?), and clothing (authoritative, or relaxed; uniforms
required?).

<Note label="Why it works">
People describe themselves aspirationally. A room is harder to fake. When the
interview says "very collaborative" and the desk faces the wall with the door
closed, the gap itself is a finding.
</Note>

When STROBE is applied, each element is scored with a small set of symbols for how
the observation compared with what the interview claimed — confirms, mildly
confirms, contradicts, mildly contradicts, or is not enough to tell — producing
an anecdotal list that sits alongside the interview notes.

## Where this idea shows up now

The "documents" are now logs, tickets, analytics dashboards, chat history, and
commit messages, but reading them is the same discipline: sample enough to
support a claim, treat blank fields and workarounds as signal, and read the
language for the team's real model of itself. Watching a recorded user session,
or shadowing support for a day, is observation; the gap between how a team says it
works in its docs and how its issue tracker says it works is a STROBE finding.
