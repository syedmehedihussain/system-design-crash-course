The safest way to find out whether a design is right is to build a piece of it and
put it in front of the people who will use it. That instinct — learn by making
something real, early, and cheaply — is what prototyping is, and it is also the
root of agile methods. This lesson covers both, in that order.

## Prototyping

A **prototype** is a partial or provisional version of a system, built to answer a
question before the full system is committed. There are four kinds, distinguished
by what they leave out.

<Figure
  src="lec06/four-prototypes.png"
  alt="Four labelled block illustrations: a patched-up prototype (uneven stacked layers), a nonoperational prototype (a solid block labelled input, process, output), a first-of-a-series prototype (one large block with facility 1, 2, 3 layers), and a selected-features prototype (a block with feature 1, 3, 5 layers and gaps)."
  caption="The four kinds of prototype."
  source="Lecture 6, slide 10"
/>

**Patched-up** — a working model that has all the intended features but is
inefficient or roughly assembled. It does the whole job, just not well; you use
it to confirm the design works end to end before rebuilding it properly.

**Nonoperational** — a non-working scale model built to test one aspect of the
design, such as the screen flow or the layout, without any of the processing
behind it. A clickable mock-up with no real logic.

**First-of-a-series** — a fully operational pilot, installed at one site. Useful
when the system will eventually be rolled out to many locations and you want to
prove it in one before committing to all.

**Selected-features** — an operational model that includes some of the eventual
features but not all, built in modules so the rest can be added later. The
features that are present actually work.

### Prototyping and the life cycle

Prototyping addresses the two standing complaints about the traditional systems
development life cycle: it takes too long, and requirements are hard to pin down
on paper. But replacing the life cycle with prototyping outright has its own
risks — shaping the system before the problem is fully understood, and users or
managers mistaking a rough prototype for a finished product. In practice
prototyping is used *within* a disciplined process, not instead of one.

### Running a prototype well

Four guidelines:

- **Work in manageable modules.** Build the prototype as parts that can be
  understood and changed independently.
- **Build it rapidly.** Speed is the point. A prototype that takes months has
  lost its reason to exist.
- **Modify it in successive iterations.** Each round of feedback produces a
  changed prototype; the modularity is what makes that cheap.
- **Stress the user interface.** The prototype's job is to get users to articulate
  requirements they could not state in the abstract, and they do that by reacting
  to a screen.

A quick route to a prototype is assembling **commercial off-the-shelf** software
modules rather than writing anything.

### What it costs and what it buys

The disadvantages: a prototype is hard to manage as a sub-project inside a larger
effort, and users or sponsors can become attached to a version that was never
meant to ship. The advantages: the system can be changed early while change is
cheap, development can be stopped if it is clearly not working, and the design
that results fits how people actually work because they helped shape it.

Users have to be honestly involved — actually experimenting with the prototype
and giving open, unfiltered reactions — and their feedback is captured on a
structured **evaluation form** so it can be compared across rounds.

## Agile modelling

**Agile methods** are a family of user-centred approaches that took the
prototyping instinct and built a whole development style around it.

### Values

<Figure
  src="lec06/agile-values.png"
  alt="A four-part disc diagram with 'Agile Values' at the centre and four segments labelled communication, simplicity, feedback, and courage."
  caption="The four values agile modelling is built on."
  source="Lecture 6, slide 25"
/>

**Communication** — keep it constant and face to face. **Simplicity** — do the
simplest thing that works and no more. **Feedback** — get it fast, from working
software and from tests, and act on it. **Courage** — be willing to change or
throw away code when the feedback says to.

The principles that follow from those values: satisfy the customer by delivering
working software, embrace changing requirements, prefer face-to-face
conversation, deliver frequently, keep the design simple, provide rapid feedback,
build in quality, and review and adjust the process itself as you go.

### Activities

Agile modelling names four basic activities:

- **Coding** — the one activity you cannot skip; it is what produces the thing of
  value.
- **Testing** — automated, written to check functionality, performance, and
  conformance, and run constantly.
- **Listening** — done "in the extreme": active listening to your programming
  partner and to the customer, on the assumption you do not already know what
  they need.
- **Designing** — creating structure to organise the system's logic, done
  continuously and evolutionarily rather than all up front.

### Resource control variables

Four things can be traded against each other on a project: **time**, **cost**,
**quality**, and **scope**. You cannot fix all four; agile makes the trade
explicit by fixing time and cost, protecting quality, and letting scope flex.

### Core practices

  src="lec06/user-story-card.png"
  alt="A user story card: fields for need or opportunity ('apply shortcut methods for faster checkout'), story (if the identity of the customer is known and the delivery address matches, speed up the transaction), a list of activities (coding, testing, listening, designing) and resources (time, cost, quality, scope), each with a rating grid from well below to well above average."
  caption="A user story recorded on a card, with room to rate the activities and resources it will need."
  source="Lecture 6, slide 39"
/>

The practices that keep agile honest: **short releases**, so the system evolves in
small visible steps; a sustainable **40-hour work week**, because tired developers
produce worse work; an **onsite customer**, available to answer questions
immediately; and **pair programming**, two people at one screen, which raises
quality and spreads knowledge.

The development loop itself: listen for **user stories** (spoken descriptions of
something a user values, recorded briefly on cards), sketch a logical workflow,
write more stories, build in short iterations, and get the working result back in
front of users.

### Scrum

**Scrum** is one agile framework. It starts with a high-level plan that is
expected to change, and organises work into fixed-length iterations.

<Figure
  src="lec06/scrum-cycle.png"
  alt="The Scrum cycle: a product backlog prioritised by the product owner feeds a sprint backlog of tasks expanded by the team; an inner loop marked '24 hours' is the daily scrum meeting, an outer loop marked '30 days' is the sprint, and the sprint produces a potentially shippable product increment."
  caption="The Scrum cycle: a sprint turns a slice of the backlog into a shippable increment; the daily scrum is the inner loop."
  source="Lecture 6, slide 42"
/>

Its pieces: the **product backlog** (everything wanted, ordered by the product
owner), the **sprint backlog** (what this iteration will attempt), the **sprint**
(the fixed-length iteration itself), the **daily scrum** (a short daily
check-in), and the **demo** (showing the increment at the sprint's end).

<Note label="Lessons">
Short releases let the system evolve; pair programming lifts overall quality; the
sustainable week improves effectiveness; balanced resources and honest estimates
keep the plan real. The recurring theme is small steps with fast feedback.
</Note>

## Where this idea shows up now

Almost all of this is now just how software is built. Prototyping survives as
design mock-ups, spikes, and feature flags; "selected-features prototype" is a
minimum viable product; "first-of-a-series" is a staged rollout. The agile
values and Scrum are mainstream rather than novel. The one thing worth carrying
forward deliberately is the framing of time, cost, quality, and scope as four
variables you must consciously trade — every missed deadline is a decision not to
flex one of the other three.
