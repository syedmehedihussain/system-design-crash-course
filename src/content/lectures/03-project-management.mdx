Before anyone models data or draws a screen, someone has to decide the project is
worth doing and then organise the work so it actually finishes. That is project
management, and for a systems analyst it runs in a fairly fixed order: a problem
is named, the project is selected against other candidates, its feasibility is
tested, resources are estimated, costs and benefits are compared, the work is
broken into tasks, and the tasks are scheduled and tracked to completion.

## Where projects come from

A systems project starts as either a **problem** — something in the organisation
is not working — or an **opportunity** — something could work better, faster, or
cheaper even though nothing is broken. Analysts notice these by checking the
output a system produces, watching how people actually work, and listening to the
complaints and workarounds that build up around a bad process.

Not every problem should become a project. The ones that should tend to share a
few traits: they respond to a *systems* solution rather than a management or
training fix, they are backed by someone with authority, the timing is right, and
solving them moves the organisation toward a real goal — more profit, a stronger
competitive position, better information for decisions, better service.

### Writing the problem down

The deliverable at this stage is a **problem definition**, and it has a specific
shape:

- A **problem statement** — a short paragraph on the current situation.
- A list of **issues** — the independent things that are wrong.
- A matching list of **objectives** — one per issue, stated so you can tell later
  whether it was met.
- **Requirements** — what any acceptable solution must do.
- **Constraints** — limits on cost, time, technology, or policy that the solution
  must respect.

The discipline of pairing every issue with an objective point-by-point is what
stops a project from drifting: if an objective has no issue behind it, someone
added scope; if an issue has no objective, the project will not fix it.

## Feasibility

**Feasibility analysis** asks whether the project can realistically be done, on
three fronts at once.

  caption="The three feasibility tests and what each one weighs."
  source="Lecture 3, slide 13"
/>

**Technical feasibility** — can the current technology, upgraded or added to,
meet the requirement? If the request needs capabilities that do not exist yet or
cannot be bought, it is not technically feasible now.

**Economic feasibility** — does the value of the investment exceed its cost in
time and money? The costs counted here include the analysts' time, the cost of
the study itself, employees' time during the project, hardware, and either
packaged software or the cost of building it.

**Operational feasibility** — will the system actually work once it is installed,
and will people use it? A system that is technically sound and pays for itself on
paper still fails if the people who are supposed to operate it will not, or
cannot.

## Estimating resources

Part of feasibility is working out what the system will need to run. Analysts
estimate current and projected **workloads** — how many transactions, of what
size, at what times — and compare the existing system's capacity against the
proposed one's.

For hardware and software the process is: inventory what exists, estimate current
and future workloads, evaluate options against those numbers, and choose. Options
include buying equipment outright, and **cloud services** — software, platform,
or infrastructure rented as a service (SaaS, PaaS, IaaS). Cloud trades control
and some security exposure for less legacy maintenance, easier acquisition, and
elastic scaling. A related decision is **BYOD / BYOT** — letting employees use
their own devices — which can lift morale and lower hardware cost but adds
security risk and complicates support.

Software itself is either **custom-built**, bought as **commercial off-the-shelf**
(COTS), or rented from a provider. Each is evaluated on effectiveness,
efficiency, ease of use, flexibility, documentation quality, vendor support, and
cost.

## Comparing costs and benefits

Benefits and costs come in two kinds. **Tangible** ones can be measured in money
— reduced processing time, fewer errors, lower staffing. **Intangible** ones
cannot be measured precisely but still matter — better decisions, improved
service, a stronger competitive position, staff morale. A good analysis names the
intangibles even though it cannot put a firm number on them.

Four techniques compare the numbers:

**Break-even analysis** finds the point where the cumulative cost of the proposed
system overtakes the cost of keeping the current one. It answers "how long until
this pays for itself?" and is the right tool when a project must be justified on
cost rather than benefit.

<Figure

  caption="Cumulative cost and cumulative benefit lines crossing at the payback point — here about three and a half years."
  source="Lecture 3, slide 52"
/>

**Payback** is the same idea stated as a single number: the time for accumulated
benefits to equal accumulated cost.

**Cash-flow analysis** looks at the direction, size, and timing of money moving
in and out over the system's life — useful for spotting a period where the
project drains more cash than the organisation can spare, even if it is
profitable overall.

<Figure
  src="lec03/cash-flow-analysis.png"
  alt="A cash-flow table by quarter: revenue, itemised costs (software development, personnel, training, equipment lease, supplies, maintenance), total costs, quarterly cash flow, and cumulative cash flow, which starts deeply negative and turns positive by year two."
  caption="Cash in and out, quarter by quarter. The cumulative row shows when the project stops costing money net."
  source="Lecture 3, slide 54"
  scroll
/>

**Present value analysis** discounts future money back to what it is worth today,
using a discount rate, so that costs and benefits spread over years can be
compared on equal terms. If the present value of benefits does not exceed the
present value of costs, the project loses money once the time value of money is
taken into account.

<Note label="Which to use">
Break-even and payback when the case is about cost and speed of return.
Cash-flow when timing of spend is the risk. Present value when the project runs
long enough that a dollar next year is meaningfully worth less than a dollar now.
</Note>

## Breaking the work down

A **work breakdown structure** (WBS) decomposes the project into tasks small
enough to estimate and assign. Each task has one deliverable — a tangible outcome
— a clear owner, and a duration. You build it by decomposition: start with the
big phases, split each into activities, split those until every leaf is something
one person can be handed and held to.

### Estimating task time

Estimates come from a few sources: **experience** with similar work, **analogy**
to a past project, and **three-point estimation**, where you take an optimistic,
a most-likely, and a pessimistic figure and combine them so a single bad guess
does not dominate. **Function point analysis** sizes a system by counting its
inputs, outputs, inquiries, internal files, and external interfaces, weighting
each by complexity, to produce a size number that can be turned into effort.

## Scheduling

A schedule turns the WBS into calendar time. Two notations do most of the work.

A **Gantt chart** puts tasks on the vertical axis and time on the horizontal,
with a bar per task drawn to scale. It shows at a glance what runs in parallel,
what is behind, and where the project is against today's date. It is simple
enough to show a non-technical sponsor.

<Figure
  src="lec03/gantt-chart.png"
  alt="A Gantt chart for a systems analysis phase: tasks such as conduct interviews, administer questionnaires, analyse data flows, introduce prototype, and prepare proposal, each a horizontal bar over a 23-week axis, shaded to show incomplete, completed, and partially completed work against a marked current week."
  caption="Tasks as bars over a week axis, shaded by progress against the current week."
  source="Lecture 3, slide 66"
/>

A **PERT chart** (also called a network diagram) shows tasks as arrows between
numbered event nodes, with each arrow labelled by its task and duration. Its
value is what the Gantt chart hides: the **order of precedence** — which tasks
must finish before which can start — and the **critical path**, the longest chain
of dependent tasks, which sets the shortest possible project duration. Any task
on the critical path that slips delays the whole project; tasks off it have
**slack**.

  src="lec03/pert-diagram.png"

## Managing risk

Projects fail for reasons that recur, and a **fishbone diagram** (Ishikawa
diagram) is a way to lay the causes out by category so they can be attacked
before they bite. The "head" is the failure; the "bones" group contributing
causes — people, process, tools, requirements — each with its own smaller causes.

<Figure
  src="lec03/fishbone-diagram.png"
  alt="A fishbone diagram with categories quality, cost, scope, and time along the top spine and design, testing, coding, and listening along the bottom, each branch listing causes such as excessive defect rate, high staff turnover, scope creep, schedule slips, fear of change, inadequate feedback from testing, and communication breakdown."
  caption="Failure causes grouped by category so each can be addressed on its own."
  source="Lecture 3, slide 73"
/>

Other tools in this area: **expediting**, deliberately speeding up critical-path
tasks by adding resources; and **earned value management** (EVM), which tracks
progress in money terms using the budget at completion, the planned value of work
scheduled by now, the earned value of work actually done, and the actual cost, so
that "we are 60 percent through the calendar" can be checked against "we have
delivered 45 percent of the value."

## The team and the proposal

A project team needs a shared work ethic, honesty, competence, and a willingness
to lead when it is your turn. Teams often run with two leaders at once — a **task
leader** who drives the work and a **socioemotional leader** who keeps the group
functioning — and it is rare for one person to be both.

The stage ends with two documents. A **project charter** states, in writing, what
the user expects, the scope, the schedule, the budget, and who is responsible for
what. The **systems proposal** is the fuller case for going ahead: cover letter,
title page, table of contents, executive summary, an outline of the study and its
methods, the detailed findings, the alternatives considered with recommendations,
and the cost-benefit analysis. Tables and graphs in the proposal are chosen to
communicate a specific point, integrated into the text rather than dumped in an
appendix, and kept to one page where possible.

## Where this idea shows up now

The vocabulary has shifted — sprints and backlogs instead of Gantt bars, story
points instead of function points, burn-up charts instead of earned value — but
the underlying moves are the same. Every planning method still forces you to
decompose work into estimable pieces, find the dependency chain that sets the
minimum duration, compare what a change is worth against what it costs, and track
delivered value rather than elapsed time. A modern feasibility spike is a
technical feasibility test with a deadline; a "definition of ready" is a problem
definition with its issues and objectives paired up.
