**Output** is information the system delivers to a user — a printed report, a
screen, a dashboard, an email, a feed to another system. It is the part of the
system most people actually see, and its design decides whether the work the
system did is usable or not.

## What good output does

Effective output:

- **Serves a specific purpose** for a person or the organisation. Output that
  exists because it always has, that nobody reads, is waste.
- **Is meaningful to its user** — in their vocabulary, at their level of detail.
- **Delivers the right amount** of information: everything needed for the
  decision, nothing that buries it.
- **Arrives where and when it is needed**, in a usable format.
- **Is chosen appropriately** as to method — the right medium for the content and
  the audience.

## Content and method are linked

You cannot decide *what* the output says without deciding *how* it is delivered,
because the two constrain each other. A number that must be broadcast to
thousands of external recipients is designed differently from one that three
analysts consult on screen.

**External output** goes outside the organisation — utility bills, statements,
paychecks, advertisements. It differs from internal output in distribution,
design (it often carries branding and legal text), and the fact that it may be
turned around and used as input (a payment stub returned with a cheque).

**Internal output** stays inside — summary reports, detailed reports, historical
reports, exception reports — and can be less formal.

The factors that decide it: who uses the output, how many people need it, where
it is needed, its purpose, the speed required, how often it is accessed, how long
it must be stored and under what regulations, the cost, and the environmental
impact. **Green IT** considerations increasingly push toward on-screen rather than
printed output.

## Output bias

The way output is presented can steer a decision without the reader realising —
and the analyst has to actively guard against it.

<Figure
  src="lec10/misleading-graph.png"
  alt="A bar chart of number of no-shows for 2011 and 2012 with a vertical axis running only from 400 to 450, so a change from about 420 to 450 looks like a threefold increase. A sticky note reads 'This diagram doesn't give the true picture.'"
  caption="A truncated axis turns a 7 percent rise into a visual tripling. Same data, different conclusion."
  source="Lecture 10, slide 15"
/>

Bias creeps in through the choice of what to aggregate and what to show
separately, the order rows are sorted in, the scale and baseline of a graph, and
which comparisons are put next to each other. Countermeasures: know the sources
of bias, involve users in designing the output, test the output with the people
who will act on it, and — when a presentation choice is unavoidably slanted — make
the reader aware of it.

## Designing reports and screens

**Printed reports** come in kinds: **detailed** (one line per record),
**exception** (only records that fall outside a normal range), **summary** (totals
and subtotals for managers), and **historical**. Each needs headings, page
numbers, dates, and clear column labels so a page makes sense on its own.

**Screen output** follows a few rules: keep the display simple, keep the layout
consistent from screen to screen, make it easy for the user to move between
screens, and keep a screen focused on one task.

For **graphical output**, decide deliberately: the purpose of the graph, the kind
of data, the audience, and the effect you want — then pick a chart type that
serves that rather than one that looks impressive.

## Dashboards

A **dashboard** puts the numbers a person needs to monitor on one screen. Good
ones:

- Give every number **context** — compared to a target, a prior period, a
  threshold.
- Show the **right level of summarisation and precision** — not six decimal
  places where two will do.
- Use the **correct chart type** and well-designed display media.
- **Highlight** what matters and **group** related data.
- Stay **uncluttered** and fit on a single screen without scrolling.

## Web and app output

Designing a website's output involves planning **structure** (every page reachable
in about three clicks), **content** (kept current, often via a content management
system), **text** (a title per page, meaningful words first), **graphics** (JPEG,
GIF, or PNG; simple backgrounds; kept few), **presentation style** (a home page,
consistent layout, cascading styles), and **navigation** (a nav bar and a
home link on every page).

Before building, the design is worked out in low-fidelity form — a **storyboard**
showing how screens differ from one another, then a **wireframe** or **mockup**
showing what appears at each position on a page without committing to final
visuals.

Designing output for **smartphones and tablets** adds constraints: design the
output again for a second orientation, design the icon, follow platform rules,
and expect to develop by prototyping with quick releases.

## Producing output from one source

Keeping content in a structured source such as **XML** lets one document be
transformed into many output formats. **XSLT** (Extensible Stylesheet Language
Transformations) selects, sorts, and reformats XML elements into HTML, print,
mobile, or another XML shape. **Ajax** uses JavaScript and XML to fetch small
pieces of data from a server and update part of a page without reloading it.

## Where this idea shows up now

The templating engine, the design system, and the component library are today's
answer to "produce many outputs from one source". Dashboard design guidance is
unchanged — context, the right chart, highlight and group, one screen. And output
bias is now a data-visualisation ethics issue with the same fixes: honest axes,
honest comparisons, and telling the reader when a choice of presentation cuts one
way.
